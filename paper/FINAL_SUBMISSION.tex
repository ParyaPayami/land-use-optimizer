\title{PIMALUOS: an open-source multi-agent framework for lot-level floor-area planning with parcel-graph learning and capacity screening}

\author[uwm]{Parya Payami\corref{cor1}}
\ead{ppayami@uwm.edu}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author}
\affiliation[uwm]{organization={School of Architecture and Urban Planning, University of Wisconsin--Milwaukee},
  city={Milwaukee}, state={WI}, country={USA}}

\begin{abstract}
Upzoning decisions are made lot by lot, yet the tools planners use to explore them rarely combine parcel data, the interests of different stakeholders and the capacity of local infrastructure in one reproducible workflow. We present PIMALUOS, an open-source Python package that (i) builds a multi-relational parcel graph from NYC MapPLUTO and learns lot embeddings with a self-supervised graph attention network; (ii) lets five stakeholder agents (residents, developers, planners, environmental and equity advocates), each trained with proximal policy optimisation, propose floor-area changes that are aggregated by weighted plurality voting under hard zoning bounds; (iii) screens every plan with vectorised planning-scale models of traffic (BPR), combined-sewer load (Rational Method) and winter-solstice shadowing, and repairs violations; and (iv) explores trade-offs with NSGA-III. An LLM retrieval-augmented extractor for district floor-area limits is evaluated against the limits published in MapPLUTO. On \NParcels{} Manhattan lots and \NSeeds{} random seeds, the verified PIMALUOS plan adds \PimAddedFAVerM{} million sq\,ft of floor area, compared with \BuildoutVerAddedFAM{} million sq\,ft for a capacity-repaired zoning build-out; we report ablations of the graph encoder, capacity feedback and individual stakeholders, a voting-game analysis of the consensus rule, and the cost of the stakeholder process relative to single-objective baselines. All results in the paper are regenerated by one command from public data.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%% ===========================================================================

Cities increasingly use lot-level zoning changes to add housing and jobs, and every such change redistributes costs and benefits among residents, property owners, the municipality and the environment \cite{batty2013new}. Planners need to know, for a candidate set of lot-level floor-area changes, how much floor area is added, which local systems it strains, who bears the risk and how the outcome depends on whose preferences prevail. Existing open tools address parts of this question: street-network and accessibility packages \cite{boeing2017osmnx,sevtsuk2025madina}, imagery and greenness pipelines \cite{ito2025zensvi,mahajan2024greenr}, land-use microsimulation \cite{waddell2002urbansim}, interactive planning tables \cite{alonso2018cityscope}, spatial multi-objective land-use allocation \cite{stewart2004genetic,cao2011spatial} and, more recently, learned planners based on adversarial or reinforcement learning \cite{wang2020reimagining,zheng2023spatial,qian2023aiagent}. None of these combines parcel-level public data, explicit stakeholder agents and infrastructure capacity checks in a single reproducible package.

PIMALUOS fills that gap with four layers (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}): \emph{Sense} (a multi-relational parcel graph), \emph{Knowledge} (retrieval-augmented extraction of district limits), \emph{Reason} (graph embeddings, stakeholder agents, voting and many-objective search) and \emph{Verify} (planning-scale capacity screens with plan repair). The decision variable is the floor-area ratio (FAR) of each lot; land use is held at its existing MapPLUTO value. The capacity models are deliberately coarse screens that can be evaluated for all lots at every learning step; they are not traffic assignment, hydraulic or ray-traced shadow models, and we do not describe the framework as physics-informed machine learning in the sense of \cite{karniadakis2021physics}.

The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item an open-source, tested Python package that runs from a MapPLUTO file to verified lot-level FAR plans, with every experiment and manuscript asset regenerated by one command;
\item a multi-agent formulation in which five stakeholder utilities, defined from public parcel data, are learned with parameter-shared PPO and aggregated by an explicit voting rule, together with an analysis of that rule as a game;
\item vectorised traffic, sewer and shadow screens and a terminating repair loop that make capacity feedback available both as a learning signal and as a post-hoc verification;
\item an evaluation on all of Manhattan against single-objective baselines, ablations and NSGA-III fronts, and an extraction benchmark for LLM-RAG against district limits published by the city.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
%% ===========================================================================

\paragraph{Land-use optimisation} Multi-objective land-use allocation with evolutionary algorithms is well established \cite{stewart2004genetic,cao2011spatial}; it typically assigns land-use classes to grid cells under aggregate objectives and does not represent stakeholders as agents. We retain this tradition in our NSGA-III layer \cite{deb2014nsga3,blank2020pymoo} and use it as a reference for the multi-agent plans.

\paragraph{Learned planners} Wang et al.~\cite{wang2020reimagining} generate land-use configurations with adversarial learning; Zheng et al.~\cite{zheng2023spatial} lay out land use and roads in urban communities with deep reinforcement learning on a graph representation; Qian et al.~\cite{qian2023aiagent} let stakeholder agents vote on land-use readjustment within a consensus-based multi-agent reinforcement learning framework. PIMALUOS is closest to the last of these, but differs in its decision (lot FAR on a whole borough of real parcels rather than land-use types), in coupling the agents to infrastructure screens, and in being released as a tested package with a full reproduction pipeline.

\paragraph{Graph learning for cities} Graph neural networks are widely used for urban prediction \cite{jin2024spatio,jiang2022graph,wang2022deep} and land-use sensing \cite{yao2017sensing}. We use graph attention \cite{velickovic2018gat} with relation-level (semantic) attention \cite{wang2019han} and masked-feature self-supervision \cite{hou2022graphmae}, implemented in PyTorch Geometric \cite{fey2019pyg,paszke2019pytorch}.

\paragraph{Multi-agent RL} Parameter-shared PPO is a strong baseline for cooperative multi-agent problems \cite{schulman2017ppo,yu2022mappo}; see \cite{zhang2021marl} for an overview and \cite{chu2020marl} for an urban application at scale.

\paragraph{LLMs and zoning} LLMs can answer questions about zoning documents at scale \cite{bartik2023costs,li2025urban}; retrieval augmentation \cite{lewis2020rag} grounds answers in the source text. We evaluate extraction of district FAR limits against the values the city itself publishes, which makes the benchmark reproducible.

\paragraph{Digital twins and screening} Urban digital twins pair data with models for scenario testing \cite{batty2018digital,albalkhy2024digital}. Our Verify layer uses classical planning-scale relations: the BPR link performance function \cite{bpr1964traffic}, the Rational Method \cite{kuichling1889relation} and solar-envelope reasoning \cite{knowles2003solar}.

\section{Data and study area}\label{sec:data}
%% ===========================================================================

We use MapPLUTO release \PlutoRelease{} \cite{dcp_mappluto}, restricted to Manhattan (BoroCode 1) and reprojected to NAD83 / New York Long Island (EPSG:2263, US feet). Of \NParcelsRaw{} Manhattan lots, \NParcels{} with positive lot area are used. MapPLUTO provides, among other fields, built FAR, the maximum allowable residential, commercial, community-facility and manufacturing FAR of the lot's zoning (exclusive of bonuses), land use, zoning district, assessed values, unit counts and FEMA flood-zone flags. Because these maxima are alternative caps for different uses, the lot's zoning maximum is their maximum, $F^{\max}_i = \max(\text{ResidFAR}_i, \text{CommFAR}_i, \text{FacilFAR}_i, \text{ManuFAR}_i)$. The higher residential cap available only when qualifying affordable housing is provided (AffResFAR) is not included, so plans are bounded by market-rate zoning.

Existing floor area is \ExistingFAM{} million sq\,ft; floor area at every lot's zoning maximum (retaining existing bulk above it) is \ZoningCapFAM{} million sq\,ft. \LotsHeadroom{} lots have headroom, and \LotsAboveMax{} lots already exceed their zoning maximum (legally non-complying bulk). Node features are engineered only from MapPLUTO columns and geometry (geometry, built form, regulation, assessed value, distance to the nearest open-space lot and to Grand Central, and an 11-way land-use one-hot); heavy-tailed variables are log-transformed and all are standardised. Features that are constant in the study area are dropped automatically, leaving \NFeatures{} of \NFeaturesEngineered{} engineered features (listed in \ref{app:features}).

\subsection{Decision space}
Each lot $i$ has existing FAR $f^0_i$ and upper bound $u_i=\max(F^{\max}_i, f^0_i)$. A plan is a vector $f$ with $f^0_i \le f_i \le u_i$: plans add floor area, never demolish it, and never exceed zoning (zoning compliance is a hard bound, so zero zoning violations hold by construction rather than being an outcome). Agents act in $H$ steps of size $\delta$: $f_i \leftarrow \mathrm{clip}(f_i + (a_i - 1)\delta,\, f^0_i,\, u_i)$ with $a_i\in\{0,1,2\}$ (decrease, keep, increase), $\delta=0.5$ and $H=10$. A decrease retracts an earlier increase.

\subsection{Parcel graph (Sense)}
All lots are nodes of a single type; five relation types are built and each is symmetrised and de-duplicated (Table~\ref{tab:graph}). Spatial adjacency connects lots whose boundaries touch within 1~ft, weighted by shared boundary length over perimeter. Proximity connects each lot to its $k=8$ nearest lots within 300~ft (weight $1/(1+d/100\,\text{ft})$); it is a distance relation, not a line-of-sight computation. Functional similarity keeps those $k$-nearest pairs with identical MapPLUTO land use. Street frontage links each lot to its two nearest lots with the same street name (house number removed, street numbers kept) within 600~ft. Regulatory coupling links each lot to its five nearest lots in the same zoning district within 1{,}000~ft.

\subsection{Graph encoder}
Let $x_i$ be the standardised features and $\mathcal{R}$ the relations. Each of two layers applies a relation-specific graph attention convolution \cite{velickovic2018gat} with four heads and the edge weight as edge attribute, and combines relations with semantic attention \cite{wang2019han}:
\begin{gather}
z^{(r)}_i = \mathrm{GAT}_r(h, E_r)_i,\qquad \tilde h_i = \sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}} \beta_r z^{(r)}_i,\\
\beta_r = \operatorname*{softmax}_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i} q^\top \tanh\big(W z^{(r)}_i\big)\Big),
\end{gather}
followed by a residual connection, layer normalisation, ELU and dropout ($p=0.2$). A linear projection gives 128-dimensional embeddings (hidden width 256). The encoder is pre-trained by masked feature reconstruction \cite{hou2022graphmae}: at every epoch 15\,\% of the training nodes have their features replaced by a learned mask token and a decoder reconstructs them (mean squared error). Ten per cent of nodes are held out and never used as reconstruction targets during training; we report their masked reconstruction error and keep the parameters with the lowest validation error (AdamW, learning rate $10^{-3}$ with cosine decay, up to \GnnEpochs{} epochs, early stopping after \GnnPatience{} epochs without improvement).

\subsection{Capacity screens (Verify)}\label{sec:screens}
\paragraph{Building envelope} A FAR increase is first accommodated within the existing number of floors by increasing footprint coverage up to 0.8 (or the existing coverage if higher); further floor area is added as height (11~ft per floor).

\paragraph{Traffic} Peak-hour vehicle trips per lot are floor area times a land-use-specific rate (Table~\ref{tab:params}). Trips are summed over square cells of 1{,}320~ft (a traffic-zone proxy). Cell capacity is proportional to the street frontage of its lots, with one constant calibrated so that the median existing volume-to-capacity ratio (V/C) is 0.7, and is floored at the capacity at which the cell's existing demand runs at that V/C. Without the floor, frontage alone left \ExistingTazOverRaw{} of \NTaz{} cells over capacity under existing conditions in Manhattan, where avenues and transit carry far more than lot frontage suggests; with it, the existing network is assumed to serve existing trips. The BPR travel-time index $1+\alpha(V/C)^{\beta}$ with $\alpha=0.15$, $\beta=4$ \cite{bpr1964traffic} is reported; a cell is a \emph{traffic violation} if V/C exceeds 1 and increased relative to existing conditions. Because capacity is calibrated to existing demand, only relative stress is meaningful.

\paragraph{Sewer} Peak combined-sewer load per 2{,}640~ft catchment cell is the Rational-Method storm flow $\sum_i C_i I A_i$ \cite{kuichling1889relation} plus peaked sanitary flow proportional to floor area. Runoff coefficients combine roofs and the lot's unbuilt surface (Table~\ref{tab:params}). The existing system is assumed to carry existing load plus 10\,\% headroom; a catchment exceeding that is a \emph{sewer violation}.

\paragraph{Shadow} At winter-solstice solar noon (altitude $90^\circ-\varphi-23.44^\circ$) a building of height $H$ casts a shadow of length $H/\tan(\text{altitude})$ due north. Lot $j$ is shaded by lot $i$ if $j$ is among $i$'s 24 nearest lots, lies north of $i$ within that length, overlaps $i$'s east--west extent, and is lower than $i$ \cite{knowles2003solar}. A plan's \emph{shadow violations} are lots shaded in the plan but not under existing conditions.

\paragraph{Equity proxies} No census data are joined. Residents are residential floor area times 2 persons per 1{,}000~sq\,ft. Green space per resident is the open-space lot area in a lot's $3\times3$-cell neighbourhood divided by its residents; we report its resident-weighted Gini coefficient. Lots are \emph{displacement-vulnerable} if they are residential and in the bottom quartile of assessed value per unit \cite{chapple2016forewarned}; displacement exposure is the share of added floor area on such lots.

\subsection{Stakeholder agents (Reason)}
Each stakeholder type $a$ has one actor--critic policy (two hidden layers of 64 tanh units) shared over all lots. The state of lot $i$ is its embedding (128) concatenated with five dynamic signals: $f_i/u_i$, the V/C of its traffic cell, the sewer utilisation of its catchment, the number of lots it newly shades, and its cumulative FAR change. Every agent proposes an action for every lot; the proposals are aggregated by weighted plurality with equal weights (0.2), and ties resolve to ``keep''. Utilities follow Eqs.~(\ref{eq:res})--(\ref{eq:eq}), with $\Delta f_i=f_i-f^0_i$, $s_i$ the residential share of floor area, $c_i$ the coverage, $\lambda$ the capacity-feedback weight ($\lambda=1$; $\lambda=0$ in the ablation) and weights listed in the code (\texttt{UtilityWeights}):
\begin{align}
U^{\text{res}}_i &= w_1\, s_i \Delta f_i - \lambda w_2\, \mathrm{Cong}_i + w_3\, \mathrm{Green}_i \label{eq:res}\\
U^{\text{dev}}_i &= f_i / u_i - \lambda\gamma\, \mathrm{Viol}_i \\
U^{\text{pla}}_i &= w_1\, \mathrm{Tax}_i + w_2 (1 - \lambda\, \mathrm{Cong}_i) - \lambda w_3 \max(0, \mathrm{Sewer}_i - 1)\\
U^{\text{env}}_i &= -w_1 (c_i - c^0_i) - \lambda w_2\, \mathrm{Shade}_i - w_3\, \mathrm{Flood}_i \max(0,\Delta f_i)\\
U^{\text{eq}}_i &= -w_1\, \mathrm{Vuln}_i \max(0,\Delta f_i) - w_2\, \mathrm{Gini} \label{eq:eq}
\end{align}
where $\mathrm{Cong}_i$ is the V/C excess of a violating traffic cell, $\mathrm{Green}_i$ green space per resident relative to its existing median, $\mathrm{Viol}_i$ indicates that lot $i$'s added floor area contributes to any violation, $\mathrm{Tax}_i$ is $\Delta f_i$ times assessed value per building square foot over its city median, $\mathrm{Shade}_i$ the number of lots newly shaded by $i$, and $\mathrm{Flood}_i$, $\mathrm{Vuln}_i$ flood-zone and vulnerability indicators. Rewards are per-step utility increments, so an episode's return equals the change in utility from existing conditions. Each agent is trained with PPO \cite{schulman2017ppo} and GAE \cite{schulman2016gae} (clip 0.2, $\gamma=0.99$, GAE $\lambda=0.95$, four epochs, minibatches of 4{,}096 lot-steps, entropy coefficient 0.01, learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$ decaying linearly to zero, \MarlIters{} episodes of $H=\MarlHorizon{}$ steps). The final plan is a greedy roll-out of the trained policies.

\subsection{Verification and repair}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:repair} removes added floor area that causes violations. It is applied to every plan, including baselines, so that plans can be compared before and after verification.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Verification and repair}\label{alg:repair}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $f \gets \mathrm{clip}(f, f^0, u)$; $t\gets0$
\Loop
  \State evaluate screens; \textbf{if} no traffic, sewer or shadow violations \textbf{then return} $f$
  \State $B\gets$ lots with $\Delta f_i>0$ in a violating cell or catchment, or newly shading a lot
  \State \textbf{if} $B=\emptyset$ \textbf{then return} $f$
  \State $\rho \gets 0.5$ if $t<30$ else $0$; \quad $f_B \gets f^0_B + \rho (f_B - f^0_B)$; \quad $t\gets t+1$
\EndLoop
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Voting-game analysis}\label{sec:game}
For \NashLots{} lots sampled from those with headroom, we treat the vote on each lot as a five-player game around the final plan: each agent's payoff for outcome $o\in\{\text{decrease},\text{keep},\text{increase}\}$ is its utility for that lot when only that lot's FAR is moved by $o$, evaluated exactly with the screens and measured relative to ``keep''. We compute the sincere outcome (each agent votes for its preferred outcome), the utilitarian optimum, and all pure Nash equilibria among profiles in which no agent votes for its strictly worst outcome (without this refinement every profile in which no voter is pivotal is an equilibrium). Because welfare can be negative, we report welfare losses in utility units and a price of anarchy \cite{koutsoupias1999worst} on welfare shifted so that the worst outcome is zero, $\mathrm{PoA}=(W_{\text{opt}}-W_{\min})/(W_{\text{worst NE}}-W_{\min})\ge1$, undefined when the worst equilibrium is also the worst outcome.

\subsection{Many-objective search}
NSGA-III \cite{deb2014nsga3,blank2020pymoo} searches the same space, with decision $x_i\in[0,1]$ and $f_i=f^0_i+x_i(u_i-f^0_i)$, minimising (1) negative added floor area, (2) the summed traffic V/C excess and sewer over-utilisation, (3) newly shaded lots and (4) added floor area on vulnerable lots. A population of \ParetoPop{} with \ParetoRefDirs{} Das--Dennis reference directions runs for \ParetoGen{} generations, either from a random population (\emph{cold}) or with half the population initialised from the status quo, the PIMALUOS plan before and after repair, and the repaired build-out plus Gaussian perturbations (\emph{seeded}). Normalised hypervolume, relative to a reference point derived from the status quo and the build-out, is recorded per generation. The knee is the front member closest to the ideal point after min--max normalisation.

\subsection{LLM-RAG extraction of district limits (Knowledge)}\label{sec:rag}
Zoning Resolution text \cite{nyc_zr} matching the MapPLUTO release is split into overlapping chunks (1{,}200 characters, 200 overlap), embedded and indexed (FAISS inner product or exact search). For each district, chunks are ranked by embedding similarity with a lexical boost for chunks containing the district code, and the LLM is asked for the maximum residential, commercial and community-facility FAR excluding bonuses (for height-factor districts, the highest achievable value), as JSON validated with Pydantic. The reference is the modal ResidFAR, CommFAR and FacilFAR that MapPLUTO reports for lots in the district that are not in a special district, have no commercial overlay and are not split between districts (districts with at least five such lots). An answer is correct if it is within 0.01 of the reference; we report accuracy with Wilson 95\,\% intervals, coverage and mean absolute error, and save every prompt, retrieved source and answer.

\section{Experimental design}
%% ===========================================================================

\paragraph{Plans compared} \emph{Status quo}; \emph{random} ($H$ uniformly random steps); \emph{rule-based} ($+\delta$ on lots below 70\,\% of $u_i$ at every step); \emph{zoning build-out} ($f=u$, the single-objective floor-area maximum); \emph{PIMALUOS}; and four ablations: \emph{no GNN} (agents see standardised raw features), \emph{no capacity feedback} ($\lambda=0$ in all utilities), \emph{single agent} (the planner alone, no voting) and \emph{no equity agent}. Every plan is evaluated before and after repair; the repaired build-out is a capacity-aware greedy baseline.

\paragraph{Seeds and statistics} The GNN and all learned plans are run with \NSeeds{} seeds; edge-type ablations with \NSeedsAbl{} (up to \AblEpochs{} epochs each); NSGA-III with \NSeedsPareto{}. We report mean $\pm$ standard deviation across seeds. Seeds, not lots, are the independent replicates, so we do not apply lot-level significance tests.

\paragraph{Computation} All experiments ran on \Hardware{} with PyTorch \TorchVersion{}. Building the graph takes \TimeGraphS{}~s; one GNN pre-training epoch \GnnSecPerEpoch{}~s; one PPO iteration \MarlSecPerIter{}~s on average over the five variants; one edge-ablation configuration \AblMinPerConfig{}~min; and one NSGA-III run \ParetoMinPerRun{}~min. The full run amounts to about \TimeTotalH{}~h of computation. It was executed in resumable segments: training state is checkpointed every 10 GNN epochs and every 5 PPO iterations, and an interrupted stage resumes bit-for-bit (tested in the package).

\subsection{Graph and representation}
The graph has \NEdgesUndirected{} unique undirected relations (\NEdgesDirected{} directed edges; Table~\ref{tab:graph}); \NIsolated{} lots have no edge of any type. On held-out lots the pre-trained encoder reaches a masked reconstruction error of \GnnValLoss{}, compared with \GnnMeanPredLoss{} for predicting the training-set feature mean and \GnnNoGraphLoss{} for the same architecture without graph relations (Fig.~\ref{fig:gnn}, Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).
Graph context carries most of the signal: because a masked lot's own features are hidden, the model without relations can do no better than the feature mean, and the full encoder reduces that error by more than half. In the single-seed ablation, removing functional similarity raises the error most, and removing regulatory coupling raises it slightly; removing spatial adjacency, proximity or street frontage leaves it unchanged, so those relations are redundant given the others and are not individually supported by this experiment. Spatial adjacency alone recovers only part of the benefit. Validation error was still falling at the last epoch (best epoch \GnnBestEpoch{} of \GnnEpochs{}), so the encoder is not fully converged; Table~\ref{tab:ablation} says nothing about downstream benefit, which the No-GNN ablation in Table~\ref{tab:main} tests.

\subsection{Plans}
Table~\ref{tab:main} compares all plans. Before repair, the zoning build-out adds \BuildoutAddedFAM{} million sq\,ft and causes \BuildoutTrafficViol{} traffic, \BuildoutSewerViol{} sewer and \BuildoutShaded{} shadow violations; after repair it retains \BuildoutVerAddedFAM{} million sq\,ft. PIMALUOS adds \PimAddedFAM{} million sq\,ft before and \PimAddedFAVerM{} million sq\,ft after repair (\PimRepairIters{} repair iterations), with \PimTrafficViol{} traffic, \PimSewerViol{} sewer and \PimShaded{} shadow violations before repair and \PimDispShare{}\,\% of added floor area on vulnerable lots. Relative to the repaired build-out, the repaired PIMALUOS plan provides \PimVsBuildoutVerPct{}\,\% floor area; relative to the no-GNN ablation, \PimVsNoGnnVerPct{}\,\%. Without capacity feedback, agents add \NoCapAddedFAM{} million sq\,ft and newly shade \NoCapShaded{} lots before repair; without the equity agent, \NoEquityDispShare{}\,\% of added floor area falls on vulnerable lots. Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff} places the plans in the floor-area/shadow plane together with the NSGA-III fronts, and Fig.~\ref{fig:map} maps the repaired PIMALUOS plan.
Four observations follow from Table~\ref{tab:main}. (i) Capacity feedback matters before repair: without it, and likewise with the planner alone, agents add far more floor area and cause roughly twice as many traffic violations and many more newly shaded lots; traffic violations and newly shaded lots are also higher per unit of added floor area, although sewer violations are not. Because these plans also overshoot by more, repair leaves them with more floor area than PIMALUOS, so feedback makes the agents conservative rather than more efficient at the margin repair rewards. (ii) After repair, PIMALUOS retains about a third less floor area than the capacity-aware greedy (\PimVsBuildoutVerPct{}\,\%). Floor area is the greedy's only objective, so this is the cost of the stakeholder process, not a failure. (iii) The GNN changes where floor area goes, not how much survives repair: with embeddings the plans newly shade fewer lots, by more than the seed spread, but cause somewhat more traffic violations (within the seed spread), and repaired floor area is indistinguishable from No GNN (\PimVsNoGnnVerPct{}\,\%). (iv) Removing the equity agent does not increase the vulnerable share of added floor area, and no learned plan places a smaller share on vulnerable lots than the rule-based baseline; with this utility, the equity agent does not measurably reduce displacement exposure. Before repair, PIMALUOS and its No-equity variant newly shade the fewest lots of all plans that add floor area; after repair every plan is free of traffic, sewer and shadow violations.

\subsection{Learning dynamics}
Fig.~\ref{fig:marl} shows episode returns per stakeholder.
Returns rise quickly over the first 20 iterations and flatten afterwards, but have not fully converged by iteration \MarlIters{}: averaged over seeds, the last ten iterations still change the stakeholders' returns by a few per cent, upward for the resident, developer and planner and downward for the environmentalist and equity advocate. The environmentalist's and equity advocate's returns are negative throughout, i.e.\ the consensus plans leave them worse off than existing conditions, as expected when other stakeholders gain from added floor area.

\subsection{The vote as a game}
Of \NashLots{} sampled lots, \NashNontrivial{} have outcomes that differ in welfare. On these, the sincere outcome is the utilitarian optimum on \NashShareSincereOpt{}\,\% of lots, with a mean welfare loss of \NashLossSincere{} utility units; the worst refined equilibrium loses \NashLossWorstNE{} on average, with \NashMeanNE{} distinct equilibrium outcomes per lot. Where defined (\NashPoaDefined{}\,\% of lots) the median price of anarchy is \NashMedianPoA{}.
The plurality vote is efficient on most but not all lots: on roughly a quarter of the sampled lots sincere voting does not pick the utilitarian optimum, and strategic voting can make outcomes worse still, since the worst refined equilibrium loses more welfare on average than sincere voting. Because the median price of anarchy is one, these losses are concentrated on a minority of lots where stakeholders disagree strongly; equilibria are often not unique. Weighted plurality is therefore a reasonable aggregation rule for this setting, but not a welfare-maximising one.

\subsection{Sensitivity to voting weights}
Holding the trained policies fixed, Table~\ref{tab:voting} varies the developer's voting weight, with the remaining weight shared equally. Raising the developer's weight above the default increases added floor area and traffic violations until the developer's vote alone decides most lots (from 0.4 the results change little), yet it reduces the number of newly shaded lots; lowering it to 0.1 leaves floor area almost unchanged but shades more lots. Shading depends on which lots grow rather than on how much floor area is added, so voting weights shift the spatial pattern of a plan as much as its size.

\section{Software}
%% ===========================================================================

PIMALUOS (v0.2.0, MIT licence) is a Python package (\texttt{pimaluos.core}, \texttt{.models}, \texttt{.physics}, \texttt{.knowledge}) with a command-line interface:
{\small
\begin{verbatim}
pip install -e ".[dev,rag]"
pimaluos run --config configs/paper.yaml \
    --pluto MapPLUTO.zip --out results/paper
pimaluos rag-benchmark --pluto MapPLUTO.zip \
    --zr-dir zr/ --provider openai --out results/rag
pimaluos report --results results/paper \
    --rag results/rag --out paper/generated
\end{verbatim}}
\texttt{run} writes a manifest (data file, feature list, configuration, versions, hardware, timings), per-seed metrics, training curves, game analysis, NSGA-III fronts and every plan; \texttt{report} writes all figures, tables and the numbers quoted in this paper. The test suite (unit tests and an end-to-end run on a small synthetic city) runs in continuous integration on Python 3.10--3.12; exact package versions are pinned in \texttt{requirements-lock.txt}. New cities require a YAML column mapping and recalibrated screen parameters.

\section{Discussion}
%% ===========================================================================

\paragraph{What the framework is for} PIMALUOS makes explicit three things that lot-level upzoning studies usually leave implicit: whose objectives drive a plan (agents and voting weights), which local systems a plan stresses (screens), and how much floor area is given up to satisfy both (comparison with the repaired build-out). Its outputs are candidate plans and diagnostics for deliberation, not recommendations.

\paragraph{Limitations}
(1) The screens are coarse. Traffic capacity is calibrated to existing demand and frontage, sewer capacity to existing load, and shadows are evaluated at one instant against nearby lots only; all thresholds are assumptions (Table~\ref{tab:params}) and results are relative. (2) Equity indicators are proxies from assessed values and open-space lots; no demographic, rent or tenure data are used, so the displacement proxy cannot identify who is at risk. (3) Land use is fixed; only floor area changes, and use-specific limits (e.g.\ residential FAR in a commercial district) are collapsed into one maximum. (4) Zoning limits come from MapPLUTO, which excludes bonuses and special-district rules; the RAG layer is evaluated but not used to drive plans. (5) Stakeholder utilities and their weights are modelling choices, not elicited preferences. (6) Only Manhattan is studied; transfer requires new data mappings and recalibration. (7) The training budgets are modest: GNN validation error and the agents' returns were still changing at the end of training, so longer training may change results.

\paragraph{Ethics} Automated planning tools can lend false precision to contested decisions and can accelerate displacement if used to target low-value housing. Plans should be reviewed with affected communities, and the displacement proxy should be replaced by local vulnerability data before any policy use.

\section{Conclusion}
%% ===========================================================================

PIMALUOS is an open, tested and fully reproducible framework that links parcel data, stakeholder agents and infrastructure screens for lot-level floor-area planning. On Manhattan it produces verified plans whose costs and trade-offs relative to single-objective and many-objective baselines are reported transparently, across seeds and ablations.
